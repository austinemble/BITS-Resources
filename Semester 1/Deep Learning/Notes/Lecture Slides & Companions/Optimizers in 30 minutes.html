What to notice

1. On the deck’s bowl, plain GD wins. The curvature ratio is only 4, so η = 0.1 is nearly ideal. That is why the slide-29 and slide-34 comparisons look so grim for RMSprop and Adam. Now drag k up to 40 and watch the ranking flip: GD must shrink η below 1/k to stay stable, then crawls along w₁.

2. GD diverges when η > 1/k. Along w₂ each step multiplies the error by (1 − 2kη). At k = 4 that crosses −1 once η passes 0.25. The “Blow up GD” preset shows it.

3. Adam and RMSprop move about η per coordinate per step, whatever the gradient size. Near the minimum they wiggle at that scale. That is the real reason adaptive methods need a decaying schedule at the end.

4–11minutes

Meet the optimizers

Every method fixes one complaint about the one before it. If you can recite the complaints, you can rebuild the formulas.

  1. Batch GD is slow (one update per epoch, all data in memory) → mini-batch SGD: about N/B updates per epoch, GPU-friendly, B ∈ {32…256}.
  2. SGD zigzags across ravines → Momentum: keep a velocity, so consistent directions add up and sideways wobble cancels.
  3. Momentum overshoots because it reads the slope where it is, not where it is going → Nesterov: peek ahead, then read the slope.
  4. One η for every weight → AdaGrad: divide by the square root of the accumulated squared gradients.
  5. AdaGrad’s sum never shrinks, so η → 0 → RMSprop: use an exponential moving average instead of a sum.
  6. RMSprop has no velocity → Adam: add momentum (first moment) and bias correction.
  7. L2 penalty gets scaled by the adaptive denominator → AdamW: apply weight decay directly to the weights.
  8. A constant η can’t both explore and settle → schedules (next block).

Personalities (use these as memory hooks)

Gradient descentA hiker who reads the slope under their boots and takes a fixed-size step. Honest, predictable, easily stuck on a ridge.
MomentumA heavy shopping cart. Hard to turn, wonderful downhill, shrugs off small bumps. With β = 0.9 it averages roughly the last 10 gradients.
NesterovThe same cart, but the driver looks ahead and brakes before the dip.
AdaGradA hiker whose tiredness never resets. Great for rare features early on, but eventually too tired to move.
RMSpropA shock absorber. It senses how rough each direction has been lately and shortens the stride there. It forgets old terrain.
AdamA car with cruise control and shock absorbers: momentum for direction, RMS scaling for stride length, and a warm-up correction so the first few kilometres aren’t off.

11–16minutes

Learning rate schedules

Start bold, finish gentle. Pick a schedule, drag the epoch slider, and read off η.

Quick facts

Step decay: η₀·γ^⌊t/S⌋. Simple and readable; the classic CNN choice (÷10 every 30 epochs).

Exponential: η₀·e^(−λt). Smooth, but rarely the best performer in practice.

Cosine annealing: ηmin + ½(ηmax − ηmin)(1 + cos(πt/T)). Fast early drop, long gentle tail. Reaches ηmin only at t = T.

Warmup: ramp linearly for the first few epochs, then decay. Early on, Adam’s v̂ is a noisy estimate and big batches amplify instability, so a full-size η at step 0 can wreck the weights.

Standard modern recipe: AdamW + warmup + cosine decay + gradient clipping at 1.0. That is what most transformer training runs look like.

16–22minutes

Practice: stabilise, debug, stop

Gradient clipping

Norm clipping rescales g to length τ when ‖g‖ > τ, so the direction is kept. Value clipping clamps each component, which can bend the direction. Example: g = [30, 40], τ = 5 → ‖g‖ = 50, so g becomes [3, 4].

Batch size

Small batches give noisy gradients (a mild regulariser) and many updates. Large batches are GPU-efficient but need a scaled-up η plus warmup. Linear scaling rule: ×k batch → ×k η. Batch 64 at η = 0.05 → batch 512 at η = 0.4.

Stopping

Set a max-epoch ceiling and add early stopping on validation loss with patience 5–10. Restore the best checkpoint. Saving the best model matters as much as stopping early.

One recipe to take away (PyTorch)

opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.01)
warm = LinearLR(opt, start_factor=0.01, total_iters=warmup_steps)
cos  = CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=total_steps - warmup_steps, eta_min=1e-5)
sched = SequentialLR(opt, schedulers=[warm, cos], milestones=[warmup_steps])

for x, y in loader:
    loss = F.cross_entropy(model(x), y)

    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    opt.step()
    sched.step()          # per iteration, not per epoch

Beyond the slides (tap to open)

Why bias correction works

Starting from m₀ = 0, the weights on past gradients in mₜ add up to 1 − β₁ᵗ, not 1, so early moments are too small. Dividing by (1 − β₁ᵗ) restores the scale. At t = 1 you divide by 0.1, which is why the slide turns m = 0.8 into m̂ = 8. By t ≈ 50 the correction is negligible for β₁ but still matters for β₂ = 0.999 (it takes ~1000 steps).

Adam’s step is scale-free

When gradients are consistent, m̂/√v̂ ≈ ±1, so each weight moves about η per step no matter how large its gradient is. Slide 34 shows exactly this: both weights drop 0.05 every iteration. It is also why Adam’s loss on the toy problem falls slowly: the step size is capped by η, not helped by the steep gradient.

Two momentum conventions

Sum form (slides, PyTorch SGD): v ← βv + g. EMA form: v ← βv + (1−β)g. In sum form the steady-state step is 1/(1−β) = 10× the raw gradient step for β = 0.9, which is why the slide-20 example needed a smaller η than plain GD. When you copy hyperparameters between libraries, check which form is used.

Nesterov, written precisely

The lookahead point is θ − ηβv (the slide writes θ − βv, dropping η, to keep the picture simple). Same cost as momentum, one extra gradient position. PyTorch: SGD(..., momentum=0.9, nesterov=True).

AMSGrad, Nadam, RAdam, Lion, Adafactor

AMSGrad keeps the running max of v so the effective step never grows (fixes a convergence counter-example for Adam). Nadam = Nesterov + Adam. RAdam rectifies the noisy early variance of the adaptive term, an alternative to warmup. Lion uses only the sign of a momentum average, so it keeps one state tensor instead of two. Adafactor factorises v to save memory on very large models.

SGD vs Adam: who generalises better?

The deck says SGD+Momentum often wins on vision. A common explanation is that SGD’s noise steers toward flatter minima, and Wilson et al. (2017) reported adaptive methods generalising worse on some tasks. AdamW narrowed the gap, and AdamW dominates language models. Treat it as a tuning-budget question, not a law.

Saddles beat local minima in high dimensions

For a point to be a local minimum, every one of millions of directions must curve upward. Random-ish critical points are overwhelmingly saddles (Dauphin et al., 2014). Momentum and minibatch noise are what push you off them. Slide 8 draws a “saddle” on a 1-D curve, but a true saddle needs at least two dimensions.

Find η with a range test; try one-cycle

LR range test (Leslie Smith): raise η exponentially over a few hundred iterations, plot loss versus η, and pick a value roughly an order of magnitude below where loss starts to blow up. One-cycle ramps up then anneals down within one run and often trains faster. SGDR restarts cosine cycles to hop between basins.

Large-batch toolkit

Linear scaling + warmup (Goyal et al., 2017, ImageNet in an hour), then layer-wise methods LARS and LAMB when batches reach tens of thousands. Gradient accumulation emulates a big batch on small memory: sum gradients over several micro-batches, then step once.

A peek at second-order methods

Newton’s step θ − H⁻¹g jumps to the minimum of a quadratic in one move and fixes ill-conditioning outright. But H has n² entries, so deep nets use cheap approximations: K-FAC, Shampoo, or L-BFGS for small problems. Adam’s diagonal v is the poor person’s curvature estimate.

Weight decay hygiene and mixed precision

Usually exclude biases and normalisation gains from weight decay. With fp16 training, scale the loss to avoid gradient underflow, and unscale before clipping, otherwise your clip threshold is meaningless.

If you remember only five lines

  1. Momentum remembers direction; RMSprop remembers size; Adam remembers both.
  2. Learning rate matters most; tune it before anything else.
  3. Adam/AdamW is the default; SGD+momentum when you can afford tuning.
  4. Warmup, then cosine decay; clip gradients for RNNs and transformers.
  5. Watch curves and gradient norms; stop early; keep the best checkpoint.
