Attention: let the model look back at what matters

Study notes for Module 8 of the BITS Pilani Deep Neural Network deck, rebuilt as a 30-minute session with live labs. You squeeze a sequence into one vector and watch it break, then drag a query around and watch weights shift, then wire up three tiny attention heads by hand. Everything below computes in your browser.

0–4minutes

The bottleneck: one vector for everything

An encoder-decoder squeezes the input into a single context vector c = hT. How much can one vector of d numbers actually carry?

Toy model: each token is a random unit vector in d dimensions. The fixed context is the sum of the n token vectors; the decoder tries to name which n of 100 possible tokens were present by their dot product with c. Attention instead keeps all n vectors and looks the right one up. The fixed vector degrades once n passes a fraction of d, because the items interfere with each other (superposition). Attention never degrades this way, but pays for it in memory: n × d numbers instead of d.

Encoder-decoder in four lines

encoder:  ht = φ(Whh ht−1 + Whx xt + b),  context c = hT
decoder:  s0 = c,  st = φ(W st−1 + W′ yt−1 + b′)
output:  P(yt | y<t, c) = softmax(Wo st + bo)

This solves the T ≠ T′ problem (a 10-word question can have a 1-word answer), but all information must pass through c. Training uses teacher forcing (feed the true previous token); inference feeds the model’s own previous prediction, so errors compound. That mismatch is exposure bias.

The fix, in one sentence: don’t make the decoder rely on one c. Let it build a fresh context ct at every step, as a weighted sum over all encoder states. That is attention.
4–9minutes

Query, key, value, by hand

Attention is a soft database lookup. The query asks, the keys advertise, the values are what you get back, blended by how well query and key match.

  1. Score: ei = score(q, ki) for every key.
  2. Normalise: αi = softmax(e)i, so the weights are positive and sum to 1.
  3. Blend: c = Σ αi vi. A weighted pool: max pooling picks one, average pooling weighs equally, attention weighs by relevance to the query.

Lab B: why divide by √dk

Random query against 10 random keys, components with zero mean and unit variance. Raw dot products have variance d, so as d grows the softmax collapses onto one key and its gradient vanishes.

The bars show the biggest attention weight (the uniform baseline for 10 keys is 0.10) and “gradient health” Σα(1−α), which is the size of the softmax Jacobian: near 0.9 is healthy, near 0 means the softmax is saturated and learns almost nothing. Scaling by 1/√dk restores variance 1 at any dimension.

9–13minutes

Bahdanau attention in a translation

The 2014 breakthrough: at every decoder step t, score every encoder state against the previous decoder state, softmax, and take a weighted sum.

  1. Alignment scores: et,i = vᵀ tanh(Ws st−1 + Wh hi)
  2. Weights: αt,i = softmax over i of et,i
  3. Context: ct = Σi αt,i hi (hi comes from a bidirectional RNN: [forward; backward], so each position sees both sides)
  4. Decoder update: st = RNN([yt−1; ct], st−1)
  5. Output: P(yt | y<t, x) = softmax(Wo[st; ct])

Lab C: read an alignment matrix

Hindi → English, with the weights from slides 43 and 51 (the remaining mass is filled in by hand). Pick a target word to see which source words it attends to and the context vector it receives, using toy 2-d encoder states.

Notice the matrix is not a diagonal: Hindi puts the verb last, so “is” attends to the final word hai, and “very good” attends to bahut achchhi in the middle. Handling reordering like this is the reason attention helps translation.

Training and inference

Training: teacher forcing, cross-entropy L = −Σ log P(y*t | y*<t, x), Adam with warmup and decay, dropout on states and attention weights. Inference: greedy (argmax each step) or beam search (keep the top k hypotheses, k ≈ 5), recompute a fresh ct each step, stop at an end token. Attention costs O(T × T′).

Lab D: three hand-wired heads

Real heads learn their projections. Here I wired three by hand so you can see what “different heads, different relations” means. The sentence is the deck’s own: The customer complained because he received wrong order. All three run the genuine formula softmax(QKᵀ/√dk) with dk = 2.

Query wordStrongest keyWeight
Head A: coreferencePronouns look for animate nouns, so he → customer. Rows with nothing to find park their weight on the first word, a habit real models also show (“attention sink”).
Head B: previous wordUses only position. The query is the sinusoidal encoding of position i−1, the key is the encoding of position j, so their dot product peaks at j = i−1. This is the deck’s “PE(pos+k) is a linear function of PE(pos)” property at work.
Head C: verb → whoVerbs look for the people in the sentence: complained and received look at customer (and at he when the mask allows).

Things to try

Causal mask on: the matrix goes lower-triangular; each word sees only itself and the past. Positional encoding off: head B collapses to uniform weights, because it has no position to read. Shuffle on, positional encoding off: the patterns of heads A and C simply permute with the words, and the check line reports 0.0000. That is permutation equivariance. Turn positional encoding on and the check fails, because position now matters.

Which one when

Self-attention: contextual representations, long-range dependencies. Cross-attention: align two sequences (decoder to encoder). Masked: anything generated one token at a time, including time-series forecasting without peeking at the future. Multi-head: essentially every Transformer.

19–23minutes

Position, and what attention costs

Self-attention has no sense of order, so position must be injected: x̃i = xi + pi.

PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 100002i/d)    PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 100002i/d)    ωi = 1/100002i/d

Lab E: the sinusoidal code

Heat map: rows are positions, columns are dimensions. The left columns flicker fast, the right columns barely change. Similarity plot: PE(p)·PE(q) is highest at q = p and falls off with distance, so nearby positions look alike. Wavelength plot: the first pair (i = 0) has ω = 1, the fastest oscillation; the last has the slowest. This is the reverse of what slide 64 says. Shift check: rotating each sine/cosine pair by kωi maps PE(pos) exactly onto PE(pos+k), for every pos, so “look k back” is a fixed linear map the model can learn.

Learned vs sinusoidal: BERT and GPT-2 learn one vector per position (flexible, but capped at the training length). Sinusoidal codes can be computed for any position, though models rarely extrapolate well beyond lengths they trained on.

Self-attention is cheaper than an RNN layer while n < d, and costlier beyond that. Its real price is the n × n attention matrix, so memory grows quadratically with length.

Beyond the slides (tap to open)

The derivation behind √dk

If the components of q and k are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, then q·k = Σ qjkj has mean 0 and variance dk. Dividing by √dk gives variance 1 regardless of dimension. Lab B measures exactly this effect.

A Transformer block, end to end

Multi-head self-attention, then add the input back (residual) and layer-normalise, then a position-wise feed-forward network, then residual and layer norm again. Dropout appears on the attention weights and on the sublayer outputs. This is where Module 11’s layer norm and dropout live.

KV cache, and multi-query attention

At inference an autoregressive model does not recompute keys and values for old tokens: it caches them, so each new token costs O(n·d) instead of O(n²·d). The cache is large, so multi-query and grouped-query attention share one set of keys and values across several query heads to shrink it.

Relative positions: RoPE and ALiBi

Rotary embeddings (RoPE) rotate q and k by position-dependent angles so that q·k depends only on the offset i−j, the same idea as Head B and the shift check, built into the score. ALiBi adds a penalty proportional to distance directly to the scores. Both extrapolate to longer sequences better than absolute codes.

Beating the n² memory wall

FlashAttention computes exact attention in tiles so the full n × n matrix never sits in slow memory: same answer, far less memory traffic. Approximate alternatives restrict who may attend to whom (sliding windows, sparse patterns) or replace the softmax with a kernel (linear attention) to get O(n).

Is attention an explanation?

Heat maps are tempting, but high weight does not always mean high influence on the output: different weight patterns can give the same prediction (Jain & Wallace, 2019). Treat attention maps as hints, not proof of reasoning.

Softmax temperature

Dividing scores by a temperature τ before the softmax sharpens (τ < 1) or flattens (τ > 1) the weights. The 1/√dk in scaled dot-product attention is a fixed temperature chosen to keep the softmax out of saturation.

Why “Attention Is All You Need” mattered

Dropping the recurrence removed the sequential bottleneck: all positions train in parallel on GPUs and any two positions are one hop apart. The price was O(n²) cost and the need for explicit positions. Today’s language, vision (ViT), speech and multimodal models are mostly stacks of these blocks.

23–26minutes

Deck watch-outs

I recomputed the deck’s numeric examples (the gold-price context 6,261 and the dot-product scores check out) and compared its claims with the papers. These are worth fixing in your own notes.

Slide 32
Softmax of the scores [0.58, 1.14, 0.44] is [0.276, 0.484, 0.240], not [0.21, 0.62, 0.17]. The printed values correspond to roughly doubled scores. Lab A shows the correct numbers.
Slide 62
“Attention(X) = Attention(PX)” is not right as written. Self-attention is permutation-equivariant: Attention(PX) = P·Attention(X), so the output rows are reordered along with the inputs. It is invariant only after a pooling step. The conclusion (word order must be injected) still stands; Lab D’s check line demonstrates it.
Slide 64
“Lower dimensions = lower frequency” is reversed. With ωi = 1/10000^(2i/d), dimension pair i = 0 has the highest frequency (ω = 1, wavelength 2π), and the last pairs have the lowest (wavelengths in the tens of thousands). Lab E plots it.
Slide 53
“The token at position 5 can only attend to positions 1–4” contradicts the mask formula on the same slide, which allows j ≤ i. A token may attend to itself, so position 5 sees positions 1–5.
Slide 22
“Secondary attention on ‘capital’ (α₄ = 0.15)”: the 0.15 belongs to the last token, α₈ (“national capital”). α₄ = 0.03 is the first “capital”, in “financial capital”.
Slide 12
The degradation beyond about 30 tokens is attributed to Sutskever et al. (2014), whose LSTM actually coped well with long sentences (they reversed the source order to help). The length-degradation finding is from Cho et al. (2014), and Bahdanau et al. show it for their no-attention baseline.
Slide 43
“Strong diagonal pattern” is not true for Hindi → English: Hindi is verb-final, so “is” aligns with the last word and “very good” with words in the middle. Off-diagonal alignment is exactly what attention captures (Lab C).
Slide 47
Multi-head is listed as a fourth “type” beside self, cross and masked, but it is orthogonal: self, cross and masked attention can each be multi-head.
Slide 60
The CNN path length O(log n) assumes dilated or hierarchical convolutions; a plain stack of kernel-k layers gives O(n/k).
Slides 37, 61, 63
“3–5× faster”, “10–100× faster training” and “generalises to any sequence length” are rules of thumb. Speed-ups depend on hardware and length; sinusoidal codes can be evaluated at any position, but models seldom extrapolate to unseen lengths well.

If you remember only five lines

  1. A fixed context vector is a bottleneck; attention builds a new context ct = Σ αi vi at every step.
  2. Weights come from softmax(score(q, k)); scale dot products by 1/√dk so the softmax doesn’t saturate.
  3. Self (same sequence), cross (decoder to encoder), masked (no future), multi-head (parallel patterns).
  4. Attention is order-blind (permutation-equivariant), so add positional encodings.
  5. O(1) paths and full parallelism, paid for with O(n²) time and memory.
